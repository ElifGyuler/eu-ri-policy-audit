# Why Does the EU Fund Israeli Defence-Linked Research?

**Strategic autonomy, foreign control, and EU research funding, 2014–2026**  
**Elif Gyuler | Utrecht University | August 2026**

> Text version of the final policy brief. Figures and interactive visualisations are available in the project notebook and `index.html`.

EU research and defence funding combines international collaboration with safeguards for ethics, security, and strategic autonomy. This brief compares Israel and Ukraine under the same Horizon Europe association framework and uses Russia only as a benchmark for how quickly EU research cooperation can be restricted through a different legal route. The analysis identifies a persistent funding asymmetry and a transparency question in the European Defence Fund concerning EU-established entities controlled by non-associated third-country owners.

## Key messages

- Problem statement — Israel and Ukraine entered Horizon Europe association within two months under the same formal participation framework, yet their EU-funded project portfolios differ substantially. The common legal framework therefore cannot by itself explain the divergence. Geopolitical alignment remains an important alternative explanation; this brief examines whether technological dependency provides an additional mechanism.

- Why Russia is included — Russia is not treated as a comparator to Israel or Ukraine. Its role is narrower: the Commission restricted research cooperation rapidly after the 2022 invasion, demonstrating institutional capacity for fast action. Those measures relied on a different legal route from Article 14 beneficiary-compliance measures, so Russia is a speed-and-capacity benchmark rather than proof that the same clause was available in the Israeli case.

- What the annual data show — Israel records 4.7 times as many EU R&I project participations as Ukraine across the 2014–2025 period. A comparison of 2019–2021 with 2022–2024 finds no statistically detectable difference in Israeli annual approvals, but annual data cannot isolate the effect of October 2023 itself. The 2023 total of 258 projects is therefore an event-year observation, not a post-October treatment estimate.

- Portfolio composition — Israeli-linked project titles sit further from the EU policy-language baseline than Ukrainian titles in the text analysis, with the largest gap inside the EDF. This is a descriptive portfolio difference; it does not by itself establish why funding decisions were made.

- Foreign-control finding — The EDF dataset contains 18 projects involving EU-registered entities under Israeli ownership. Intracom Defense is 94.5% owned and 100% voting-controlled by the state-owned Israel Aerospace Industries. EDF Article 9 generally restricts third-country control but allows derogations where Member-State-approved guarantees are provided; the public project records reviewed here do not disclose the content of those guarantees.

- Core argument — The ownership and supply-chain evidence raises a strategic-autonomy question rather than proving that cooperation is inherently counterproductive. Israeli-controlled participation may provide European capability or technology-transfer benefits, but those gains should be assessed against whether ownership, control, and underlying external dependencies actually shift over time.

- Policy options — Clarify criteria for Horizon Europe research-security restrictions; increase transparency around EDF Article 9(4) control guarantees; establish standing parliamentary review of security-relevant R&I partnerships; and require cross-horizon assessment of both capability gains and dependency risks before strategic technology partnerships are renewed.

## 1. The issue

EU research policy combines international collaboration with safeguards concerning ethics, research security, and strategic interests. Israel and Ukraine entered Horizon Europe association within two months in 2021 under the same formal participation framework, yet their funding trajectories differ substantially. The comparison does not equate the conflicts involving the two countries; it asks why comparable programme rules coexist with different funding outcomes.

Project-level SEDIA data show 258 new Horizon Europe projects involving Israeli entities in 2023 and 74 involving Ukraine. Russia provides a separate benchmark: in early March 2022 the Commission halted new Horizon Europe agreements with Russian organisations and suspended payments under existing research contracts, with subsequent EU restrictive measures further limiting participation by Russian public bodies. That sequence shows that research cooperation can be restricted quickly, but it does not establish that the same legal clause applies to Israel.

The obvious alternative explanation is geopolitical alignment: Russia is treated as an adversary, while Israel is a close partner. This brief does not treat that distinction as irrelevant. Its narrower question is whether technological dependency and sectoral integration add explanatory power once the formal Horizon Europe association framework is held constant between Israel and Ukraine.

The question matters because the EU is simultaneously trying to reduce external technological dependencies and build sovereign capacity in AI, cybersecurity, semiconductors, and defence. Cooperation with external partners can help close capability gaps and transfer knowledge; it can also preserve external ownership or supply-chain reliance. The policy problem is therefore not cooperation versus autonomy, but how the EU evaluates those two effects over time.

## A note on the comparison: why Israel, Ukraine, and Russia?

This brief rests on a specific comparative design, and it is worth being explicit about the logic behind it before presenting the evidence, not least because it is the point most likely to be misread.

Israel and Ukraine are compared because one important procedural condition is the same: both signed Horizon Europe association agreements within two months in late 2021 under Article 16 of the Horizon Europe Regulation and possess the same formal participation rights. This does not make the two conflicts equivalent, nor does it hold every political variable constant. It simply rules out association status itself as an explanation for the funding divergence. Geopolitical alignment, historical partnership depth, sectoral composition, and technological interdependence remain competing or complementary explanations.

Russia is not part of that Most Similar Systems comparison. It is a descriptive capacity benchmark: the Commission moved quickly to restrict research cooperation after the 2022 invasion. The legal route was different, however. In a 2026 parliamentary answer, the Commission explicitly distinguished the Russian restrictive-measures context from Article 14, which requires beneficiary-specific non-compliance. Russia therefore shows that fast programme-level action is possible; it does not prove that the same contractual mechanism should have been used in the Israeli case.

## 2. What the data show

### 2.1 A persistent, decade-long funding gap

Israel and Ukraine provide a useful procedural comparison because both entered Horizon Europe association within two months under the same formal participation framework. That shared status does not imply that funding levels should be equal, but it allows one institutional explanation—different association rules—to be set aside. The remaining divergence must be assessed against other factors, including sectoral composition, geopolitical alignment, and accumulated technological integration.

They do not. Israel’s total participation across Horizon 2020, Horizon Europe, Digital Europe and the EDF stands at 2,474 projects and €9.43 billion since 2014; Ukraine’s stands at 523 projects and €2.38 billion. The 4.7-times gap is not the product of one anomalous year, it holds, with only modest variation, across every year from 2014 to 2025. Figure 1 also plots Russia on the same axis, for reference; Section 2.2 explains why Russia’s trajectory, not Israel’s or Ukraine’s, is the one that changes.

The gap is not evenly spread across programmes. It is narrowest in Digital Europe, a civilian capacity-building instrument where Ukraine actually receives more projects than Israel (29 versus 1), consistent with the dedicated solidarity provisions in Ukraine’s association agreement. It is widest, by a large margin, in the European Defence Fund, where the ratio reaches 18-to-1. This pattern, a moderate gap in civilian research, an extreme gap in defence-adjacent research, is the first sign that something more specific than general partnership breadth is driving the asymmetry.

### 2.2 Annual continuity across 2019–2024, in a system that has responded before

Russia remains useful here only as a descriptive benchmark for speed. Figure 1 shows a sharp break in the Russian series after the Commission restricted research cooperation in 2022. Israel and Ukraine, by contrast, continue along annual trajectories without a comparable collapse. Because the Russian measures were taken through a different legal route, the comparison demonstrates institutional capacity for rapid action rather than operation of the same legal instrument.

A Welch’s t-test comparing Israeli project approvals in 2019–2021 with 2022–2024 finds no statistically detectable difference (t = −0.22, p = 0.85), with the same broad conclusion from a non-parametric check. Ukraine rises over the same two periods, although the six annual observations provide limited statistical power. These are period comparisons, not a pre/post test of October 2023: the 2022 observations precede the event, and the 2023 total contains both pre- and post-October months. The 2023 Israeli count of 258 projects is therefore reported descriptively as an event-year observation.

The evidence here documents different institutional outcomes; it does not by itself establish that identical measures were legally required in each case.

A text analysis of project titles, comparing Israeli and Ukrainian portfolios against the EU’s own normative vocabulary, drawn verbatim from the Horizon Europe Regulation, the EU’s research security recommendation, and the European Defence Industrial Strategy, shows that Israeli-linked portfolios diverge substantially further from that vocabulary than Ukrainian portfolios do, in every programme, at every level.

Ukraine’s distinctive vocabulary, energy, sustainability, digital transformation, resilience, closely tracks the EU’s own normative language almost word for word. Israel’s distinctive vocabulary clusters around dual-use AI, autonomous systems, quantum sensing, and biomedical-defence crossover technology. The gap is largest inside the EDF itself: Israeli-linked defence titles diverge even from the EDF’s own already security-oriented baseline, driven by a concentration of explicitly operational-military terms (armoured, tactical, deployments) essentially absent from the rest of the EDF portfolio.

## 3. How Israeli-controlled EU entities can participate in the EDF

The EDF rules distinguish between place of establishment and third-country control. Entities established outside the EU or associated countries cannot be EDF recipients. At the same time, Article 9(3) generally restricts EU-established recipients that are controlled by a non-associated third country or entity, while Article 9(4) permits such participation where guarantees approved by the Member State of establishment are made available to the Commission and satisfy specified security conditions. The issue documented here is therefore not a missing ownership rule, but how that derogation operates and how visible its safeguards are to external scrutiny.

Cross-referencing SEDIA participant records against corporate-ownership data identifies Intracom Defense AE, registered in Athens, as the main Israeli-controlled participant. Since May 2023, Intracom has been 94.5% owned and 100% voting-controlled by Israel Aerospace Industries (IAI), a wholly state-owned enterprise of the Government of Israel. Intracom coordinates 17 of the 18 EDF projects linked to Israeli ownership; those projects have a combined project budget of €438.4 million, while Intracom’s own direct EU contribution is €29.9 million. A Belgian Elbit Systems subsidiary appears in one additional project as a non-coordinating participant.

Five of Intracom’s 18 projects were signed before IAI’s May 2023 acquisition and 13 afterward. Public ownership records establish equity and voting control, but the public project records reviewed for this brief do not disclose the content of any Article 9(4) guarantees, how the Commission assessed them, or what constraints were placed on the exercise of control for individual actions. The publicly listed board composition therefore cannot be used to infer a nominee arrangement. The accountability question is the transparency and application of the derogation, not an assumption that ownership went unchecked.

They matter here because they complicate a simple diversification narrative: cooperation with an Israeli-controlled EU entity may still connect European projects to a technology ecosystem that is deeply integrated with US firms and policy. Whether that cooperation ultimately builds European capacity is an empirical question that the project-level data alone cannot answer.

## 4. Strategic autonomy: diversification or dependency relocation?

One interpretation of the funding pattern is normative inconsistency: the EU applies different instruments across different external relationships. A second is geopolitical alignment. A third, examined here, is structural dependency. These explanations are not mutually exclusive, and the project-level data cannot rank them conclusively.

But there is a sharper problem specific to this moment. Since 2016, and especially since the Von der Leyen Commission’s strategic autonomy agenda, the EU has explicitly targeted its own dependency on American and Chinese technology as a strategic vulnerability. The European Defence Industrial Strategy states plainly that 78% of EU defence procurement flowing outside the Union, with 63 percentage points of that going to the United States alone, is unsustainable. The Chips Act’s recitals describe semiconductor dependency as a threat to defence capacity and digital sovereignty.

Israeli participation is concentrated in domains central to the EU’s strategic-autonomy agenda, including AI, autonomous systems, cybersecurity, and semiconductors. On one reading, cooperation with Israeli firms is a pragmatic way to access mature capabilities while reducing direct reliance on larger suppliers. Figure 6 complicates—but does not disprove—that interpretation: IAI is itself embedded in US defence-industrial relationships. The policy question is therefore whether European participation generates durable capability transfer and greater control over time, or whether external ownership and supply-chain dependence persist beneath EU-based project structures.

Related debates in EU cloud and digital-sovereignty policy raise a broader analytical point: formal European establishment does not always answer questions about technological control, jurisdiction, or underlying infrastructure. Those cases are not legally identical to the EDF, which contains an explicit third-country-control regime. They nevertheless reinforce the need to examine strategic autonomy below the level of registration alone.

It is also worth noting that this failure sits specifically at EU institutional level, not across Europe as a whole. Spain has suspended arms exports to Israel; France excluded a major Israeli defence firm from a national exhibition after public pressure. Individual member states have shown they are willing to act. The absence of comparable EU-level action reflects a specific gap in EU accountability architecture, not a Europe-wide absence of political will, a distinction that matters because it points to an institutional fix rather than a diplomatic one.

The scale of the underlying economic relationship helps explain why that institutional gap has proven so durable. EU member states held an estimated €72 billion in foreign direct investment in Israel in 2023, nearly twice the equivalent US figure, while bilateral trade reached roughly €43 billion in 2024. Israeli technology firms’ presence in Europe grew, rather than contracted, through the period covered by this brief, with industry representatives describing Europe explicitly as a complementary market to the United States rather than a substitute for it. Set against that scale of entanglement, the R&I and defence-fund asymmetry documented here is not an isolated funding anomaly. It is one visible, measurable facet of a much larger structural integration that current EU-level accountability mechanisms were never designed to govern.

## 5. What the Commission and Parliament could do

The options below do not all use the same legal route. Some build on existing administrative powers and oversight practices; others would require greater transparency, formal guidance, or legislative change depending on how far policymakers choose to alter the substantive eligibility rules.

**1. Clarify criteria for Horizon Europe research-security restrictions. The Commission already has tools to limit participation in specified security-sensitive calls. Publishing clearer cross-country criteria for when those tools are triggered would make differentiated treatment more transparent without assuming that every geopolitical case is legally identical.**

**2. Make EDF third-country-control derogations auditable. Article 9 already addresses control by non-associated third countries and permits participation under approved guarantees. The immediate transparency option is to disclose, to the extent compatible with security constraints, the basis on which such guarantees satisfy Article 9(4). If legislators want a stricter substantive rule for majority-controlled entities or coordinator roles, that would require a legally robust amendment rather than a reinterpretation of territorial registration.**

**3. Establish standing parliamentary review of security-relevant R&I partnerships. A regular review mechanism could examine the use of research-security restrictions, third-country-control derogations, and the strategic implications of major technology partnerships without relying on ad hoc parliamentary questions after disputes arise.**

**4. Require cross-horizon capability and dependency assessment. External technology partnerships can close immediate capability gaps and may generate knowledge transfer. Before renewal in strategically sensitive domains, the Commission could assess both sides of that trade-off: whether European actors gain durable capabilities, intellectual-property leverage, and supply-chain resilience, and whether foreign ownership or control remains structurally unchanged.**

Taken together, these options would not require the EU to treat different conflicts as equivalent or to end research cooperation with Israel. They would instead make the criteria governing security-sensitive partnerships more transparent, bring third-country control and derogations into public accountability, and distinguish short-term capability gains from longer-term dependency risks.

## A note on the data and methods

This brief draws on a project-level dataset of 58,876 EU research and defence funding records (2014–2026), extracted from the European Commission’s SEDIA funding database, the underlying data infrastructure of the EU Funding and Tenders Portal, which unlike the more commonly used CORDIS database also covers the European Defence Fund. All classifications (funding asymmetry, temporal intervention, thematic divergence, corporate ownership) were produced using a transparent, rule-based methodology, cross-validated using multiple independent statistical tests, and checked against an official EU-commissioned assessment of EDF participation and independent investigative reporting where available. A full academic treatment of the methodology, validation procedures, and complete findings, including a legal-institutional analysis of the accountability gap, is available on request. Replication data and code will be made available at a public repository.
